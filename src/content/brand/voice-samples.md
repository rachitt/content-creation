## Hand-Written LinkedIn Samples

### LinkedIn 1
The demo is not the product. The product is what happens after the third weird customer input.

Most AI workflow builds look solid in the happy path.

Then a user uploads a malformed PDF, skips two required fields, changes their mind halfway through, and asks support why the output looks different from last week.

That is where the real product starts.

The work is not adding a model call. The work is building the boring system around it: validation, retries, review queues, permissions, observability, and a rollback path when the model is wrong.

Founders feel this pain when a prototype starts getting customer attention. The same demo that helped close interest becomes a liability because every edge case becomes a manual intervention.

Our rule is simple: if the workflow cannot explain, recover, or hand work back to a human, it is not ready for production.

Working on this now? Send the workflow. I will point out the riskiest part.

### LinkedIn 2
Most rebuilds do not fail because the old app was messy. They fail because the team never names what has to stay boring.

Every founder wants the new version to feel cleaner.

That is reasonable. The problem starts when a rebuild becomes a chance to revisit every product decision at once: new data model, new permissions, new onboarding, new billing, new AI layer, new dashboard.

Now the team is not replacing software. It is running a discovery process inside an engineering project.

The better move is narrower.

Keep the proven workflow intact. Identify the parts that create operational drag. Replace the weak boundaries first: brittle integrations, manual admin steps, unclear ownership between services, missing tests around revenue paths.

A rebuild should buy you speed after launch, not a prettier way to stall before it.

DM me if you are planning one and want a second set of eyes on the scope.

### LinkedIn 3
The fastest SaaS teams are not the teams with the most code. They are the teams with the fewest unclear handoffs.

Handoffs hide everywhere.

Sales promises one version of onboarding. Ops tracks a second version in a spreadsheet. Engineering ships a third version because the edge cases were never written down. Support becomes the routing layer between all of them.

This is where custom software can help, but only if it removes a decision point.

The goal is not a bigger internal tool. The goal is a workflow where the next action is obvious, the state is visible, and exceptions have a named owner.

When we audit a product, we look less at the feature list and more at the handoffs: who waits, who copies data, who approves, who fixes the weird case, who knows something broke.

That is usually where the roadmap is hiding.
